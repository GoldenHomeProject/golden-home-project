What HGTV Means by 'Mixed Metals' — And What It Actually Costs

The HGTV piece frames mixed metals as brass, chrome, and nickel coexisting in one kitchen. What it doesn't mention is that most kitchens already have two of those three finishes installed — in the faucet, the pendant lights, or the cabinet hinges — and the fastest way to 'mix metals' on a budget is to keep those fixed elements as-is and change only the cabinet hardware to a single, deliberate contrast finish.

That's the mechanism behind the $10,000-remodel comparison: a kitchen with a brushed-gold faucet and brass light fixtures reads as intentionally mixed the moment matte black pulls go on every door and drawer, because the eye reads two finishes used consistently as a design choice, not two finishes used randomly as a leftover from different renovation decades. The failure mode reviewers describe from the outdated look isn't the presence of brass — it's three or four different finishes (brushed nickel knobs, brass hinges, chrome faucet, black light fixtures) with no repeating pattern.

The practical version: audit what's already fixed and can't be swapped for $27 — the faucet, the light fixtures, the appliance finish — and pick one contrasting hardware finish that plays off it consistently. If the kitchen runs warm-toned (oak cabinets, brass faucet), matte black pulls are the highest-contrast, lowest-cost way to make that mix look chosen rather than accidental. If the kitchen already runs black and white, the same swap reinforces rather than fights the existing palette.

What Reviewers Report About Fit and Installation

Before ordering, the fit question matters more than the finish question — a pull that looks right and doesn't match the existing screw spacing means a trip back to the hardware store or new holes in the cabinet face. Reviewers consistently report two hole-spacing configurations working out of the box: standard 3-inch (76mm) and 5-inch (128mm) centers, which cover the majority of cabinets built in North America since the early 2000s.

For cabinets with nonstandard spacing — older custom cabinetry, or hardware bought as decorative single-hole knobs rather than two-hole pulls — reviewers note the swap requires either drilling a second hole or using a cover plate, and recommend measuring the existing hole spacing with a tape measure before ordering rather than assuming standard sizing. The included screws are reported as generously long, useful for thicker cabinet doors (glued MDF or reinforced fronts) but requiring trimming or swapping for shorter hardware-store screws on thin 1/2-inch cabinet doors, per multiple reviews.

On the mounting process itself: this is a screwdriver job, not a power-tool job, for a direct swap onto existing holes. The step that actually takes time isn't the installation — it's removing 25-30 old pulls first, which reviewers estimate at roughly a two-hour project for a full kitchen when going pull-by-pull with a manual or battery screwdriver.

Frequently asked

Will these cabinet pulls fit my existing cabinet holes?

Reviewers report the Ravinte 5-inch pulls fit standard 3-inch (76mm) and 5-inch (128mm) hole spacing, which covers most cabinets built since the early 2000s. Measure your existing hole spacing with a tape measure before ordering — nonstandard or single-hole knob setups will need a cover plate or a new hole.

Do I need to drill new holes to install these?

Not if your cabinets already use two-hole pulls at standard spacing. It's a direct swap: remove the old pull, mount the new one with the included screws. Reviewers flag that the included screws run long for thin 1/2-inch doors, so have shorter screws on hand as a backup.

A typical U-shaped kitchen uses 20-30 pulls across cabinet doors and drawers. The 30-pack at $26.99 covers most kitchens in a single order, with a few pulls left over for a pantry door or bathroom vanity.
