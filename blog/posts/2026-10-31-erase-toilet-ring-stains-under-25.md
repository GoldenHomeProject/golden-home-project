## Why the Ring Won't Just Wipe Off

Hard water carries dissolved calcium carbonate and, in a lot of municipal and well systems, iron. Every time water sits and evaporates — along the toilet's waterline, down the overflow holes under the rim, across a tub's faucet spout — it leaves those minerals behind as a solid deposit. That's limescale. It's chemically closer to a thin layer of rock than to dirt, which is why a sponge and dish soap move right over it without dissolving anything.

## FAQ

**Will a pumice stone scratch my toilet or tub?**

Manufacturer guidance and reviewer reports agree it's safe on glazed porcelain and ceramic tile only when kept wet during use — used dry, it can dull the glaze. It should not be used on fiberglass, acrylic tubs, or colored/textured porcelain finishes, where it can leave visible scratches.

**What actually causes the brown or yellow ring in my toilet?**

It's mineral deposit, not dirt — mainly calcium carbonate and, in many water supplies, iron or manganese, left behind each time hard water sits at the waterline and evaporates. That's why standard cleaners that target grease and bacteria often don't remove it.

**Does bleach remove limescale, or just whiten stains?**

Bleach is effective at whitening organic staining and killing bacteria, but it doesn't chemically dissolve calcium deposits the way an acidic cleaner or mechanical abrasive (like a pumice stick) does. For set-in limescale, reviewers consistently pair a bleach gel with scrubbing rather than relying on bleach alone.

**How often should I treat a toilet or tub for hard water buildup?**

Based on the maintenance routines described in reviews for the ToiletWand system and squeegee, a weekly gel-and-brush pass on the toilet plus a squeegee swipe after every shower prevents new mineral deposits from reaching the point where they need mechanical removal.

For a single toilet with a set-in ring, a $9 pumice stick plus a bottle of bleach gel ($5.24) is the lower upfront cost at roughly $14.23.
