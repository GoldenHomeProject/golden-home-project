Why Hard Water Stains Don't Come Off With Normal Cleaners

Hard water carries dissolved calcium carbonate and magnesium. Every time water sits in a toilet bowl or dries on a tub wall, a thin mineral film gets left behind. Over months, that film builds into a crust — the orange tint usually comes from iron in the water supply, the gray-white comes from straight calcium buildup. Neither is organic grime, so bleach, ammonia-based sprays, and most all-purpose cleaners don't touch it; those products are formulated to break down soap scum, bacteria, and oils, not mineral deposits.

The two things that reliably break down calcium carbonate are acid (vinegar, CLR, citric acid) and abrasion (pumice, which is a volcanic glass rated around 6 on the Mohs hardness scale — soft enough not to scratch glazed porcelain, hard enough to grind off limescale, which sits around 3 on the same scale). That hardness gap is the entire reason a $6-8 pumice stick works where a $12 bottle of spray cleaner doesn't: it's not chemistry, it's physics. Reviewers on the Pumie Scouring Stick listing consistently describe wetting the stick, wetting the stain, and scrubbing in short strokes rather than circles, which matches the manufacturer's own usage instructions and avoids leaving fine scratch lines on softer fixtures like acrylic tubs.

Frequently asked

Will a pumice stick scratch my toilet or tub?

Pumice sits around 6 on the Mohs hardness scale while glazed porcelain is harder than that, so used wet it shouldn't scratch standard porcelain. It can scratch acrylic, fiberglass, or colored/textured fixtures, which is the most common complaint in reviews — check the fixture material before scrubbing.

Do I need to use the pumice stick wet or dry?

Wet. Both the manufacturer instructions and the recurring one-star Amazon reviews agree: using it dry creates dust and increases scratch risk, while wetting the stick and the stain first lets it glide and break down mineral deposits without abrading the glaze.

What's actually causing the orange ring in my toilet bowl?

Usually dissolved iron in the water supply combined with calcium carbonate buildup below the waterline. It's a mineral deposit, not mold or rust from the fixture itself, which is why iron-fighting, acid-based, or abrasive approaches work and bleach alone typically doesn't.

Is vinegar or a pumice stick better for hard water stains?

Vinegar (acetic acid) dissolves lighter, newer mineral film but needs soak time, often 30+ minutes, and struggles with thick, years-old buildup. A pumice stick works mechanically and faster on hardened crust but requires physical scrubbing. Many reviewers use vinegar to loosen a stain first, then pumice to finish it.

How often does a toilet need the pumice stick treatment?

Reviewers with consistently hard water report doing a pumice pass every 1-3 months, with lighter maintenance (a wand or brush with cleaner) in between. Households on softened water report needing it far less often, sometimes just once or twice a year.

A hard water ring that's taken a year or more to build up doesn't need a $20 bottle of specialty spray to remove — the physics are simple: pumice is harder than limescale and softer than porcelain, which is why a $6-8 stick keeps showing up on Amazon's best-seller charts and in roundups like Real Simple's.
